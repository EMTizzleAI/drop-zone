🔴 IMMEDIATE SAFETY FIRST

In danger now? Seriously injured? Call 911.

In the U.S., 911 connects you to police, fire, and EMS. If you need an ambulance, say so. Tell the dispatcher your location, what is happening, and whether an injury or weapon is involved. If possible, move to a safer place without confronting the person harming you.

🚑 MEDICAL CARE & DOCUMENTATION

If you are hurt, get medical care.

Call EMS for an emergency injury or if you cannot get to care safely. For other injuries or sexual assault, seek prompt evaluation at an emergency department or urgent care. Tell the clinician what happened and ask that injuries, symptoms, and your description be documented accurately in your medical record.

Pressure on your neck, choking, or strangulation is a medical emergency. Internal injuries can occur without visible marks. Seek emergency medical evaluation promptly; call 911 if you have trouble breathing, swallowing, speaking, confusion, fainting, or other urgent symptoms.

A medical record can be valuable later, even when you decide not to make a police report. Ask for the visit summary and how to obtain your records safely. In some places, healthcare professionals must report certain injuries; ask about confidentiality and reporting rules when it is safe to do so.

🚓 REPORTING / YOUR SAFETY / YOUR CHOICE

Consider making a police report—even without a visible injury.

If it is safe for you, reporting threats, assaults, stalking, or repeated abuse can create a contemporaneous record. That record may help establish a pattern in later investigations, protective-order proceedings, or other legal matters. It does not guarantee any particular outcome.

You do not have to wait until you are physically injured. If danger is immediate, call 911. For a past incident without immediate danger, contact the local police department's non-emergency line or ask a domestic violence advocate about reporting with support.

Police involvement can also create risks for some survivors, including retaliation or concerns involving children or immigration status. Talk through your options with an advocate when possible. Your experience matters whether or not you report.

If you do report, ask how to obtain the incident or report number and the officer's name. Keep those details somewhere the abusive person cannot access.

📁 PROTECT THE RECORD

Document what happened, if doing so is safe.

  1. Write down dates and details. Record incidents, threats, witnesses, and what was said or done, as soon as it is safe.
  2. Preserve records. Save photos of injuries or property damage, threatening messages, call logs, and voicemails with dates and identifying information.
  3. Seek medical documentation. Even minor symptoms may matter. Request records, visit summaries, or discharge paperwork.
  4. Consider a police report. If reporting is safe for you, request an incident number and keep a copy when available.
  5. Store copies safely. A trusted person, legal advocate, or secure account accessed from a safe device may help. Avoid creating evidence on devices that are being monitored.

You do not need a “perfect” case to ask for help. Evidence can be useful later, but safety comes first.

📊 THE NUMBERS ARE REAL

Domestic violence affects millions.

In the CDC's 2023/2024 National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey, these were lifetime estimates of U.S. adults who had experienced contact sexual violence, physical violence, and/or stalking by an intimate partner:

These estimates use a specific definition, not every form of domestic abuse. Emotional abuse, coercive control, and financial abuse also matter. Source: CDC NISVS 2023/2024 Intimate Partner Violence Data Brief ↗.

📡 EMTIZZLE // FIELD NOTE

You deserve to be taken seriously.

Abuse does not need to leave a bruise to be real. If you're hurt, seek care. If you're in danger, call emergency services. If you can safely document or report what happened, those records may matter in the days, months, and years ahead.

And if reporting isn't safe right now, you can still get help. Reach out to a trained advocate and decide on a safer next step.
